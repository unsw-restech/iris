Contribute a workflow

Built an imaging analysis of your own? Offer it to IRIS, and other researchers can launch it on Katana in two clicks. You stay its workflow owner, named on its page, and you get a DOI for it that credits you and your co-authors.

Not yet

Proposals open at launch, and so do pull requests from authors outside the pilot. IRIS is still being tested, so hold yours until then; the steps below are how it will go.

Who can contribute

Anyone with a Katana account. You don't need to work at ResTech, and you don't need write access to the IRIS repo: you build on your own fork and open a pull request. Anyone who adds or changes a workflow is a workflow author.

What you get

  • An accessible workflow: researchers launch it from IRIS in two clicks, with no install, on their own data in their Katana scratch.
  • Your name on it, on the workflow's page as its workflow owner, so researchers know whose work it is and who to ask.
  • A DOI for each citable version of the workflow, through WorkflowHub, listing you and your co-authors (with ORCIDs) beside UNSW Research Technology Services. Researchers who publish results from it cite that DOI.
  • An easy link for colleagues: the workflow's page, with its Run on Katana button, is the one address you send them.
  • No paperwork: your workflow is licensed Apache-2.0, like the rest of IRIS, with no separate agreement to sign.

What gets accepted

These are the fit criteria, the only place IRIS writes them down. The maintainer reviews a workflow for safety and fit against them, never its science: you answer for that. A workflow:

  • has imaging at its core. It may also read data that complements the images, such as tables or audio, but IRIS takes no workflow without imaging.
  • fits a modality and a lab in the catalogue, such as microCT at the X-ray MicroCT Lab or MRI at BRIL. A new modality or lab needs a proposal.
  • reads one dataset: one folder in the researcher's scratch, in a layout its page documents and its form's check: validates.
  • writes a run directory through its workflow's own copy of IRIS's library, so every run is recorded and can be compared.
  • runs in its lab's environment. If it needs packages that environment lacks, or at other versions, that needs a proposal, and your pull request makes the environment's next version.
  • makes no network calls or downloads at run time.
  • is Apache-2.0 compatible, its code and everything it depends on.

When you need a proposal

Most workflows need no proposal: build it, and your pull request is where it is approved. File a proposal through the IRIS issue form first only when you need something only the maintainer can give:

  • New packages your lab's environment lacks, or at different versions.
  • A new modality or lab. A new lab's first environment is built by the maintainer from the approved proposal; a lab with no packages of its own uses an existing one.
  • Adopting an unmaintained workflow (below).

The maintainer approves a proposal with the approved label, or declines it by closing it as wontfix.

From fork to DOI

  1. Check it fits the criteria above, and propose first if you need to.
  2. Build it on a fork. Fork the IRIS repo, turn on Actions on your fork, and follow CONTRIBUTING's steps: scaffold the workflow, write its page, name yourself its owner, add any packages, and give it a test on a synthetic dataset.
  3. Prove it runs on Katana, from your own clone, as CONTRIBUTING describes.
  4. Open a pull request once IRIS's checks pass, on your fork and where CONTRIBUTING says to run the rest. The pull request is the only approval a workflow needs.
  5. Wait for a review batch. The maintainer reviews every ready pull request together, about every fortnight, when the maintainer chooses: nothing enforces it, and yours waits until the next one.
  6. Merged, or closed with a reason. A merged workflow joins the catalogue with you named as its workflow owner. A closed pull request names the criterion it failed: fix that and reopen it.
  7. Mint its DOI. Join the UNSW ResTech team on WorkflowHub (Team 472), register the workflow there and mint its first citable version.

The technical details of each step are in CONTRIBUTING.

Help while you build

The maintainer doesn't help during the build, and doesn't go back and forth on a pull request: it is merged, or closed naming the criterion it failed, and you reopen it once that's fixed. Your help is CONTRIBUTING, what IRIS's checks print when they fail, and your own coding agent, which can follow every step: the procedure is commands and checks, not judgement calls.

Being a workflow owner

Owning a workflow means answering for it. Researchers who run it come to you, not the maintainer:

  • Fix it when it breaks, and answer questions from researchers who run it.
  • Review changes to it: you are asked about every pull request that touches its folder.
  • Update it when you choose, proving it runs again each time.
  • Mint a new citable version when a change to its code or defaults changes its results, and not otherwise.

To hand it to someone else, open a pull request naming them as its owner. The maintainer can change any workflow at any time, in any detail, and the maintainer's own workflows follow these same rules.

If an owner stops answering

If someone reports a problem with a workflow and its owner doesn't answer for six months, it becomes an unmaintained workflow. An owner who loses their Katana account counts as not answering. It leaves the catalogue and can no longer be run, but its page stays, so its DOIs and the commits researchers cited still lead somewhere.

Anyone can adopt it through a proposal. The adopter fixes it, proves it runs, and becomes its workflow owner. It comes back as the same folder, under the same page and the same WorkflowHub entry.
